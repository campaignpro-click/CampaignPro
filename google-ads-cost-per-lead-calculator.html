How the calculator works

Break-even means the point where the profit from the customers your ads bring in exactly covers what you paid for the ads. The calculator works it out in three steps:

Profit per customer = revenue per customer × jobs per customer × profit margin

Break-even cost per lead = profit per customer × share of leads that become customers

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ profit margin

The "target" cost per lead shown next to your break-even number is half of it. That is a rule of thumb, not a Google Ads rule: it leaves you half of the gross profit from each lead, so there is room for overhead, slow months and your own pay.

Worked example

The form starts with this example: a customer pays $2,000 for one job, you keep a 40% margin, and 1 in 5 leads (20%) becomes a customer.

  • Profit per customer: $2,000 × 40% = $800
  • Break-even cost per lead: $800 × 20% = $160
  • Target cost per lead: half of that, $80
  • Break-even ROAS: 1 ÷ 0.40 = 2.5×, so $2.50 of revenue for every $1 of ads

If this business pays $95 per lead, it is above the target but still profitable, with about $65 of profit left per lead. At $180 per lead, every lead would lose about $20.

What to do with your number

  1. Find your real cost per lead

    In Google Ads, divide total cost by the number of conversions. Make sure those conversions are real leads, such as calls and form submissions, and not page views or button clicks.

  2. Compare it with break-even and target

    Enter it in the optional field above. Under your target is healthy, between target and break-even is thin, and above break-even loses money on each lead.

  3. If you are above break-even, fix the leaks first

    Cutting the budget rarely fixes a high cost per lead. Check conversion tracking, search terms and negative keywords, location targeting and whether the landing page matches the search.

  4. If you are well below break-even, scale carefully

    A low cost per lead can mean room to spend more. Raise budgets in steps and watch whether your cost per lead and lead quality hold up.

Common mistakes

  • Using revenue instead of profit. A $2,000 job is not $2,000 of room to spend on ads.
  • Counting every lead as a good lead. Spam, wrong-service and out-of-area calls lower your real lead-to-customer rate.
  • Ignoring repeat business. If customers rebook, raise the number of jobs per customer. Otherwise you may pass on leads that would pay off.
  • Forgetting the fee. If you pay an agency or freelancer, your all-in cost per lead is higher than what Google Ads shows.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cost per lead on Google Ads?

There is no single good number. A good cost per lead is one comfortably below your break-even cost per lead, which depends on what a customer is worth in profit and how many leads become customers. Industry averages give context: about $47 for cleaning services to about $129 for plumbing, according to LocaliQ's 2025 home services benchmarks. Your own break-even matters more than any average.

Should I enter revenue or profit?

Enter the average revenue per customer and your profit margin separately. The margin should reflect what you keep after the cost of doing the work, such as labor, materials, fuel and subcontractors, before ad spend. Break-even depends on profit, not revenue.

What is break-even ROAS?

Return on ad spend (ROAS) is revenue divided by ad spend. Break-even ROAS is 1 divided by your profit margin. With a 40% margin it is 2.5, meaning you need $2.50 of revenue for every $1 of ad spend just to cover the cost of the work and the ads.

I don't know my lead-to-customer rate. What should I enter?

Look at your last 20 to 30 leads and count how many became paying customers, then divide customers by leads. If you can't tell yet, try several rates, such as 10%, 20% and 30%, to see how much your break-even cost per lead changes.

Does the calculator include my management fee?

Only if you enter it. When you add your monthly ad spend, current cost per lead and an optional management fee, the calculator shows your all-in cost per lead and your estimated monthly profit after both.

How accurate are the results?

The results are planning estimates based on the numbers you enter. Real results move with seasonality, lead quality and how quickly you follow up, so use the output as an upper limit on what you should pay per lead rather than a forecast.
